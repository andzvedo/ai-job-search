André Azevedo Design Exercise

Research Study about Qualitative LLM Evaluation

Goal

Understand how AI engineers compare LLMs in real product contexts, emphasizing qualitative evaluation to uncover key pain points, best practices, and potential opportunities. Since the product is meant for AI engineers working with LLMs in complex, real-world scenarios, understanding how they currently evaluate models, where they struggle, and how qualitative judgment fits into their workflow helps ensure that the UX addresses actual needs and fits existing mental models and practices.

Research Method

A secondary (desk) research was conducted to base design decisions on evidence rather than assumptions. This involved reviewing articles and model comparison reports, scanning social media discussions where AI engineers share workflows and pain points (e.g., Reddit and Twitter), and analyzing how evaluation appears in product documentation. Insights were synthesized by grouping them into themes, trade-offs, and professional language around LLM quality and reliability, that informed my design decisions.

Key research questions

  • How do AI engineers work with LLMs in their day-to-day product and development workflows?
  • What factors influence how they explore, choose, and trust different models?
  • What challenges and constraints shape their evaluation and decision-making processes?
  • What tools, practices, and artifacts currently support how they test and iterate with LLMs?

Key findings

Fairness matters:

Comparisons need consistent inputs and shared setup to be trusted.

LLM choice is recurring:

Teams re-evaluate as prompts, models, and constraints change.

AI Engineers must ship + defend:

Decisions need a rationale that can be shared with stakeholders.

Workflow is fragmented:

Early tests are ad-hoc; evidence ends up scattered across chats, notes, and screenshots.

Qualitative is central:

Important product dimensions (helpfulness, tone, safety, clarity, verbosity, follow-up) don’t collapse into a single score.

Qualitative judgment is indispensable:

Trustworthy LLM evaluation depends on human-in-the-loop, descriptive assessment of coherence, relevance, appropriateness, and potential bias, dimensions that do not reduce to a single metric.

Context matters:

Model performance and perceived quality shift with prompt framing, domain context, and user intent, so evaluation must be situational rather than generic

Design principles

Fairness by design

Comparisons must minimize bias. The product enforces shared setup, same prompts, and optional blind mode so differences reflect model behavior, not configuration artifacts.

Evidence over scores

Single numbers rarely capture LLM quality. The design prioritizes notes, per-turn evidence, and qualitative rationale over aggregate scores.

Human-in-the-loop evaluation

Automated signals can scale evaluation, but human judgment is necessary for coherence, relevance, and appropriateness. The UX supports quick human feedback at the moment of impression.

Context-first evaluation

LLM quality is situational. Prompts, domain, and constraints are treated as first-class elements so results are interpreted within context, not as generic leaderboards.

Key trade-offs considered

Speed vs. rigor

Fast evaluations risk superficial judgments; rigorous ones demand more effort. The solution balances this with lightweight feedback (like/dislike) plus optional notes.

Simplicity vs. expressiveness

Too many criteria increase cognitive load. The design starts simple and allows deeper commentary only when needed.

Structure vs. flexibility

Highly structured rubrics improve consistency but can feel restrictive. The product uses “rubric-lite” signals and free-text notes to preserve evaluator nuance.

Neutrality vs. transparency

Blind mode reduces bias, but users may still want to know which model is which. The system allows controlled reveal after evaluation.

How insights informed design

  • Fragmented workflows → consolidated chat + evaluation in one place
  • Need for defensible decisions → decision trail and exportable summaries
  • Qualitative centrality → notes and per-turn judgments
  • Fairness concerns → shared config and blind mode
  • Recurring re-evaluation → repeatable runs and new sessions

User Flow

This user-flow illustrates the happy path of the solution: entering the product, configuring a comparison, choosing models, chatting and evaluating responses, and finally viewing consolidated results.

Home / Get Started

  • User understands the product and starts a new comparison via a CTA.

New Compare

  • Selects models to compare

Select Models

Settings

  • Enter System instructions, Temperature, Max Tokens, and adjusts other settings…

Chat and Evaluate

  • Sends prompts.
  • Receives responses from A and B.
  • Rates each answer (Rating + Notes).
  • Records evidence per turn.

First turn completed / Starting a new turn

  • User sees how history and evaluations accumulate.
  • Can keep chatting or Finish, to go to Results.

Results

  • Sees qualitative winner (A vs B) with confidence.
  • Reads executive summary and metric breakdown.
  • Accesses key evidence (per turn).
  • Exports report / JSON.
  • Starts a new run to repeat in another domain

What I would validate next

  • Do different evaluators reach similar conclusions using this interface?
  • Is the flow efficient enough for real workflows?
  • Do written notes actually help users defend decisions later?
  • Do users feel more confident in model choice compared to their current tools?
  • Would AI engineers integrate this into their iteration cycle or treat it as a one-off tool?
